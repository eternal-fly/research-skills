# Schematics: turn the paper's relationships into visible structure

Use this FIGURES reference for apparatus, mechanisms, energy levels, protocols, circuits, or actual processing workflows. Complexity follows the relationships that need explanation: one clear relationship may need only one compact drawing, while several named icons with arrows may still fail to explain a mechanism. A schematic does not automatically belong in Figure 1 and does not replace quantitative evidence. When materials and tools are available, continue through drawing, inspection, correction, and delivery rather than stopping at a design brief.

## The shortest path from the paper to the artwork

1. **Define the figure's task.** Write one question the reader should be able to answer, such as “Which measurable information constrains which quantity that cannot be observed directly, and how does that determine the control?” Extract the answer from the existing text, equations, experiments, and methods, recording source locations. Establish the relationships that must survive before deciding the panel count.
2. **Make a small mapping.** For each necessary element, record “paper object/symbol → visible entity or structure → connection meaning → source.” Show inputs as actual records, fields, states, or parameters; models as actual degrees of freedom/structures; inference as fitted variables and constraints; outputs as actual observables or choices. Use generic network, grid, or chip icons only when their structure genuinely explains the question. A box labeled “physical constraints” cannot replace showing what is constrained.
3. **Put the central relationship in the main artwork.** Express the core reasoning with traceable paired objects, connections, shared coordinates, branches/merges, or local relationships. Keep only the equation terms needed to explain that relationship, with symbols near the corresponding objects. A full equation stuffed into a process box is not a visual explanation. Object shapes should preserve actual geometry, hierarchy, or state distinctions where relevant.
4. **Organize dependencies and scales.** If training and deployment use different data or update different variables, separate offline construction from online inference and identify frozen and still-changing objects. If a comparison depends on paired conditions, place the two branches side by side and mark common inputs/constraints. Add a magnified view only when both the overall apparatus and a local interaction matter; identify its anchor and the degrees of freedom being enlarged. Otherwise, draw the local relationship directly. Each group, background band, or inset must answer a specific question.
5. **Connect to evidence.** Use the same variables, conditions, colors, and line styles to connect schematic relationships with the corresponding data panels. State which relationship a panel tests, what its control changes, and what stays fixed. A new schematic can refer to an existing data figure; extra panels are not needed merely to supply another figure type. Without a calculation, draw symbolic relationships and identify the precise gap rather than drawing invented curves.
6. **Render at the actual size.** Inspect at the final 85 mm or actual double-column width, then within a complete manuscript page. If content is too dense, remove nonexplanatory objects, split reading levels, or adjust column span. Do not shrink text to squeeze every method step into one figure.

The mapping is a short working record, not a required long audit table. When the starting model, controls, data, and applicability conditions are already supplied, extract them directly. Ask only about gaps that block the scientific meaning.

## Turn the visual method into a brief and finished artwork

After mapping objects and before drawing, write a short paragraph defining six things: **layout and reading order; the shape/structure of each object class; scientific roles assigned to colors; central and supporting details; connection types and visual hierarchy; labels and known dimensions**. When a reference image exists, extract these six organizational choices from it, then rebuild them using the current material's objects. The brief constrains the drawing. For example: “Use a horizontal main path, stacked slices for features, and compact layer blocks for operators; blue for inputs, orange for the central transformation, green for outputs, and gray for supporting elements; continuous, clear main dependencies and dashed update dependencies labeled with their targets; labels close to interfaces and known dimensions shown locally.” This is a replaceable design example; reassign color roles and structures to match the current research.

### A ready-to-fill visual brief and styling starting points

Before drawing, record: `reading order/main area → object name: shape, scientific role, role color, fill/outline, label/dimensions → main/internal/supporting connections: meaning, direction, line style, and hierarchy → visual priority of central/secondary/grouping elements`. Assign at least one shape and styling role to every object class before drawing; repeated nodes do not need separate entries.

The following are **adjustable implementation starting points for this package, not upstream or APS rules**. They apply only to schematic objects; numerical curves, uncertainty bands, and color scales retain their data semantics and the technical baseline. Make an initial drawing at final physical size, then adjust after checking glyphs, density, and endpoints:

| Styling role | Starting fill and outline | Connection/information hierarchy |
|---|---|---|
| Central transformation/key component | Role color such as orange #F28E2B, solid alpha=1; dark outline around 1.1 pt | Main connections around 1.0 pt, alpha=1; the central object and its interfaces should be immediately identifiable |
| Input/intermediate representation/output | Semantically appropriate colors such as blue #4E79A7 or green #59A14F; distinguishable fills with dark outlines around 0.8 pt | Distinguish roles with layer blocks, feature slices, actual components, or other appropriate shapes, and place labels nearby; do not use circles for every class |
| Repeated internal structure | The same role color or a lighter but still clear solid fill; necessary outlines around 0.7 pt | Start internal connections around 0.7 pt, alpha=1; do not rely entirely on a transparent web of lines to explain structure |
| Supporting update/supervision | Gray #6C757D or the associated object's color; a short label identifies the target | Directed dashed lines around 0.7 pt; visually subordinate to the main path but readable at final size |
| Grouping containers/page | White or pale neutral backgrounds with light boundaries | Enclose the content without letting container color or area overpower scientific objects |

Line width encodes reading hierarchy, not numerical coupling strength; adjusted widths must still meet applicable APS requirements. Define each role color's scientific meaning for the current figure. Labels on dark fills may move just outside the object onto nearby white space or use a sufficiently contrasting text color. Never sacrifice readability to preserve a fill.

1. **Put color on scientific objects.** Give feature slices, layer blocks, components, or key nodes distinguishable mid-value fills according to their meanings. Use restrained but clearly saturated accent colors for central objects. Choose darker related colors or dark neutrals for outlines, and text that contrasts clearly with its background. Let solid/filled objects, details, and whitespace jointly establish visual weight; merely tinting hollow circles and thin lines is insufficient. Not every object needs a fill: keep containers, the page, and supporting groups white or pale neutral so colored scientific objects remain primary.
2. **Bind a limited palette to roles.** The upstream blue #4E79A7, orange #F28E2B, green #59A14F, and gray #6C757D palette is a possible starting point. State which colors represent input, processing, output, support, or other roles in the current task; these are not fixed scientific identities. Repeated objects retain the same identity across panels rather than cycling through colors. Existing manuscript color meanings take priority. Coordinate with linked data figures instead of mechanically replacing their curve colors. Retain names, shapes, or line styles as well as color, and check correspondences in grayscale and for color-vision differences.
3. **Give structure a visible form.** Represent networks as appropriate with feature planes, tensor stacks, layer blocks, operation nodes, and meaningful connections; central layers may have stronger outlines. Build physical components from their actual topology and interfaces. Stacked slices or side faces must explain hierarchy, dimensions, or physical structure, not add decorative fake 3D, depth, or unsupported layer counts. Hollow neurons are not the only network representation. Choose the form that best explains the computational object, and show numerical dimensions only when known.
4. **Give connections a hierarchy.** Continuous, clear dark or role-colored connections for main data flow and central physical relationships establish the reading path. Repeated internal connections may be lighter but must remain traceable at final size. Supporting supervision/updates use distinct line styles and explicit labels. Visual strength indicates reading hierarchy, not physical coupling magnitude unless a numerical mapping is defined. First clarify critical endpoints and arrowheads, then reduce supporting density; do not thicken every line together. Central boundaries should stand out from light grouping boundaries.
5. **Adjust value and contrast from actual renderings.** Begin with opaque object fills and clear outlines, then refine colors as needed. Data layers that convey overlap or uncertainty follow their own data meanings. Pale groups organize content; they must not turn every primary object into a faint watermark. There is no required overall fill fraction, uniform opacity, or fixed line-width threshold. Final physical size, APS requirements, and distinguishability jointly determine specific values. The fill/opacity starting points and connection hierarchy here are this package's implementation choices, not numerical standards published by the upstream source or APS.
6. **Compare every brief item with the finished figure.** After rendering, inspect at final width: Is color concentrated on meaningful objects? Do their forms show structure? Is the central mechanism noticed first? Is the main path continuous and clear? Is supporting information subordinate but readable? Do labels correspond to objects? Compare structure, colored-area weight, and hierarchy with the user's reference, not just hex codes. If the figure still looks faint, first fix primary fills and outline/connection hierarchy, then spacing. A dark background or universally thicker lines is not the default remedy. Render and inspect again after changes.

**Choose tools for the figure's task.** When image tools genuinely help explore visual concepts, shape language, or composition, turn the six-part brief into an explicit prompt and retain the generated result as a design reference. Use them only when suitable tools are available and useful. Generate numerical curves/heatmaps from data with Python or equivalent tools. Check precise circuits, scientific connections, text, and dimensions in editable vector/TikZ/SVG/Python artwork. A mixed workflow may explore composition with generated imagery and then rebuild the scientific structure; model-generated labels, topology, and values are not evidence. When the visual plan is already clear, direct vector drawing is appropriate. Writing a prompt does not replace delivering a figure.

## Compact information density: distinct roles for overview and mechanism

The default target is a **compact, layered, readable journal schematic** that exposes source-supported internal operations, objects passed between stages, and key dependencies within limited space. This is a user visual preference, not mandatory APS layout. User-provided references set the target for visual organization and density: extract their object forms, structural detail, scale hierarchy, and efficient use of space without inheriting scientific identities, numbers, or conclusions. Current materials still determine the science.

First list the objects and connections readers must follow, then organize the artwork as follows:

1. **Let the overview establish the path.** Use a compact backbone to show inputs, actual key stages, and outputs. Label transferred records, states, parameters, features, or observables near interfaces. Show defined dimensions locally when they matter to understanding. Draw actual branches for shared inputs and explicit merges for combinations; crossing lines are not automatically junctions.
2. **Open up the mechanism in the main artwork.** Put the operations that distinguish this method from a generic workflow inside nested areas of the relevant stages: necessary transformations, operators, local degrees of freedom, intermediate objects, and their dependencies. Pair short labels with actual structure so readers can see how inputs produce outputs. Large boxes containing module names or generic network icons cannot do this alone.
3. **Make supporting views add another layer of information.** Add an anchored local expansion only when the overview cannot legibly expose an essential internal relationship; retain the module name, symbols, colors, and shapes. The overview explains placement and dependencies; the expansion explains internal mechanism. Do not redraw the same process or repeat the same text. Give most space to the central mechanism. If data sources, splits, or states have no geometric relationship needing explanation, compress them into interface labels, narrow bands, or captions rather than giving them panels as large as the mechanism. Merge or remove supporting views that add no explanatory task.
4. **Make operations and boundaries traceable.** Identify sums, products, concatenations, projections, readouts, and other operations with source-supported operators or short labels. Arrows crowded together cannot imply a merge operation. In fitting/inference, measurement records and theoretical models feed a fit or comparison node separately; if measurements do not generate the theory, do not depict a “measurement → theory” sequence. Feedback requires a defined return target and evidence of an actual closed loop. If training occurs, make training data/targets, loss, and updated parameters locatable, with distinct forward-computation and supervision/update paths. Connect predictions and targets to the actual comparison/loss node, then make the update dependency from the loss to the affected parameters explicit. An isolated loss equation below the figure cannot replace these interfaces and connections. Frozen, trainable, shared, or local boundaries appear only when the method actually makes those distinctions. For multistage training, identify what is fixed and what is updated at each stage.
5. **Get density from efficient space use.** Reduce space consumed by empty frames, repeated headings, and meaningless long arrows. Align compact objects of the same kind and share their visual language. Organize nesting with pale groups or thin boundaries; use whitespace for routing, label separation, and hierarchy. Do not turn the whole figure into equal-width large cards or repeat nodes to fill space. First determine content bounds, arrow routes, and necessary spacing from readable labels and actual operations; fit containers to that content, then determine canvas height. Do not allocate full-width, equal-height bands first and populate them sparsely. Use ellipses only for source-supported repeated structure, with the repeated extent clearly identified.
6. **Protect final-size readability first.** At the intended column width, inspect the smallest text, subscripts, arrowheads, and local mechanisms. If too dense, remove redundancy, rearrange, separate layers, or increase width appropriately before considering anything else. Do not globally shrink text to pursue “high density.” There is no pass threshold for node count, panel count, fill fraction, or information per square centimeter. Simple relationships still deserve simple compact figures. Do not fabricate internal layers, loss terms, branches, feedback, or numerical panels.

Information density passes when, at final size, a reader can follow the main path through labeled interfaces and point to operations and intermediate objects inside key modules. Each expansion explains something new, and whitespace makes routing clear. More elements, smaller text, or more borders do not imply a clearer mechanism.

## Scientific objects need recognizable structure

First use the current manuscript and methods to decide whether each representation below applies; then incorporate it into the main artwork. Labels explain structure; they cannot impersonate it.

- **When a neural network is present and its computation is part of the figure's explanatory task:** show actual input samples/tensors or features, source-supported layers/operators, connections and branches, latent representations, and outputs as a traceable network structure. Choose feature planes/tensor stacks, layer sequences, node connections, local operations, and shape changes according to the objects. Readers should be able to follow input through latent quantities to output and identify where key computations occur. Large encoder/decoder boxes with letters or formulas alone are not a neural-network schematic. Expand only modules actually present in the sources; not every network has an encoder or latent bottleneck.
- **When quantum hardware/a physical implementation is present and its relationships are part of the figure's explanatory task:** show source-supported platform components or degrees of freedom, control ports/drives, actual couplings, and readout as a connected physical structure. Link controlled/measured objects to their corresponding pulses, measurement records, or subsequent computational signals, creating real interfaces between apparatus and algorithm. If the platform is known, use its known components. With only an abstract model, draw degrees of freedom and ports and label the drawing “model/schematic”; do not pretend it is a measured chip or completed deployment. A quantum circuit shows operation order and does not automatically replace requested hardware structure.
- **When structure is known but dimensions are unknown:** retain known hierarchy and connections, use symbolic dimensions, and mark illustrative repetition with explicit ellipses plus a statement that it does not specify actual layer count/width. Do not invent exact counts, wiring, or hyperparameters. Conceptual sources support conceptual structural drawings; unknown numbers do not require retreating to empty text boxes.
- **When the content does not involve a class of object:** do not add it. Nonlearning protocols do not need neural networks, and papers unrelated to quantum hardware do not need quantum apparatus. When the user explicitly requests the relationship between hardware and a network and the materials contain both, the main figure must visibly show both structures and their interfaces. A simplified substitute system or circuit-only drawing cannot fulfill that request.

Resolve structural detail and compact layout together. First choose the scientific objects that explain the mechanism and make them visually primary; then arrange local labels and short connections around readable objects. Evaluate each large blank area separately: add source-supported structure if a necessary mechanism is missing; otherwise reduce spacing, container size, or canvas size. Adding text, enlarging formulas, decorating borders, or mechanically filling gaps does not fix the issue. Preserve whitespace needed for routing and reading groups, without fixed fill-fraction or object-count thresholds.

## Method flowcharts: explain the process, not just the topic

When the task is to explain a complete method, first extract its actual dependency graph from the material. Follow data or control through it, retaining real branches, parallel operations, merges, and feedback. Determine which stages actually exist: source/physical system, input records, construction or training, fixed/update boundaries, execution/inference, measurement/prediction/scoring, and final action or judgment. Omit inapplicable stages and connect actual dependencies; this is not a generic learning-workflow template. Do not omit stages that determine how the method works merely to use fewer icons or a single row. A conceptual overview may compress detail; a method flowchart must tell readers what each step does, uses, and produces, and show its sequence/dependencies.

- **Fill four fields for every key stage:** input; internal operation or necessary structure; output; and the object transferred between stages. Fine implementation details may go in supporting figures, but structures that distinguish this paper's method stay in the main figure.
- **When a neural method includes training:** distinguish the encoder, decoder, or other actual modules. Make training data/targets, what is trained first, what is frozen, and what is trained afterward locatable. Show known structure/dimensions when they affect understanding; do not invent layer counts, widths, or loss terms. A “neural network” icon cannot cover all these roles.
- **When the method addresses a physical system:** keep the system visible and connect it to input signals, controls, and readout. Use paper-supported components/degrees of freedom to draw an abstract physical structure, explicitly identifying actual experiments, simulation validation, or an intended application. Do not portray simulations as completed hardware deployments, or remove the physical object merely because validation is simulated.
- **Separate execution from explanation:** the main path first shows the complete actual training/inference/calibration workflow. Local mechanism explanations or theoretical relationships form anchored supporting layers. Explanatory scientific insets must not displace key operational stages or imply that the algorithm actually computes derivatives or physical quantities used only for interpretation.

Completeness is not a step count. If removing an element would make readers misidentify data provenance, updated objects, forward/inverse relationships, the physical system, or the final output, keep it and express it clearly. Simple tasks such as a two-mode connectivity diagram do not need the complete method workflow.

## Choose representations by content, not icons by terminology

| Content to explain | Visible representation | Scientific distinctions to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Modes, energy levels, interactions, or transport | Modes/levels/actual ports and defined interaction lines | Coherent coupling, driving, dissipation, allowed transitions, and readout differ |
| Conditional responses and differences | Paired responses sharing inputs, with common/difference terms and combination relationships explicit | Two independent fits differ from predictions constrained by the same family |
| Inference or calibration | Observed inputs, estimated variables, fixed constraints, predicted outputs, and decision quantities | Trained parameters, online coordinates, physical parameters, and controls differ |
| Geometry/scale relationships | Actual topology, nested regions, or anchored local enlargements | Schematic and quantitative scales differ, as do physical connectivity and layout adjacency |
| Time protocols or feedback | Operation timelines, states, measurement outcomes, and conditional branches | Temporal order, information dependency, and physical action differ |

For example, a two-mode diagram may show only two defined modes, drives, exchange g, and individual losses κa and κb. That is enough if the task is merely connectivity. If the task is hybridization, link that relationship to the available spectral/response comparisons. Do not add detectors without a port mapping or realistic-looking chip wiring without hardware evidence. Complexity is not a quality target.

## Lines have types; drawings have constraints

Define every line type in the figure or caption, with local labels wherever confusion is likely:

- External drive: source → driven object, labeled with actual frequency/amplitude or other relevant quantities.
- Coherent coupling: exchange between degrees of freedom, labeled with its coupling parameter; bidirectionality, phase, or nonreciprocity must match the equations.
- Dissipation/leakage: system → corresponding environment/loss channel; draw bath feedback only if the model contains it.
- Readout: measured object → detector/record variable, labeled with the actual observable. Circuit measurements use a recognizable meter/detector symbol or an explicit “measurement” operation, identify the measurement basis, and output a classical record. A Z alone inside a box denotes a Pauli-Z gate and cannot impersonate a Z-basis measurement.
- Inference/data flow: observation → estimated variable → prediction; lines express computational dependency, not physical transport.
- Decision/feedback: score → selected control, or measurement → actual conditional operation; reconnect to the system only for a real closed loop.

Color, width, and solid/dashed styles encode only defined distinctions; without a quantitative mapping, they do not imply interaction strength. Distinguish model-prescribed relationships, operations, and inferences still to be tested. A connection is not causal evidence.

Check relevant invariants: connectivity and direction; coordinate/handedness conventions; energy-level order/allowed transitions; inputs, outputs, and variable dimensions; dimensional consistency of symbols; and symmetry/conservation/boundary conditions. Preserve the conditions for derivatives, tangents, linearization, and approximations rather than drawing them as universal identities. Trajectories, spectra, heatmaps, or surfaces with numerical meaning require traceable calculations/data. Explicitly identify purely schematic content; add “not to scale” where scale could be misread.

## Final artwork checks: visible and explainable relationships

Record these checks separately; neither substitutes for the other:

1. **Scientific meaning and rendering:** first check sources, dependencies, measurement/gate operations, and simulation/experiment identities; then check final-size text, endpoints, clipping, and exports. Passing this check establishes only accuracy and readability.
2. **Match to the user's visual target:** compare the actual finished figure side by side with the supplied visual reference. Are the main objects recognizable scientific structures? Is their internal hierarchy exposed? Is space organized compactly around them? Have the brief's role colors, primary color-fill weight, and main/supporting connection hierarchy actually been realized? Numerous text boxes, correct formulas, complete arrows, or successful export do not establish a style match. Identify remaining missing structure or large blank areas, revise the drawing, and inspect again. If the user has rejected the visual match, record that conclusion rather than overriding it with a passed technical check.

Inspect the artwork item by item at its actual insertion size, not merely the drawing plan:

- **Content:** the figure and short caption let readers identify inputs, central objects/constraints, key transformations, and outputs, and locate relationships specific to this paper. If only a few interchangeable method names remain, return to the object mapping and redraw.
- **Structure:** applicable networks, physical systems, or hardware have genuinely recognizable layers/features/components/connections. Labels and equations attach to structure rather than replace it. Unknown dimensions remain symbolic or explicitly schematic.
- **Hierarchy and density:** central scientific relationships are easiest to find; overview and expansions each add information; internal operations and intermediate objects are locatable. Group boundaries, reading order, and inset anchors are clear. Text stays near its objects, arrow endpoints and merge operations are unambiguous, and empty frames, repeated titles, and useless long corridors do not consume mechanism space.
- **Integrity:** distinguish fixed/varying, measured/inferred, schematic/quantitative, and exact/approximate content. Do not invent apparatus, responses, or control results for visual richness.
- **Correspondence:** variables and conditions agree across schematics, manuscript text, and data figures. Identity is conveyed by text, shape, or line style as well as color. Data comparisons genuinely correspond to the stated mechanism.
- **Readability:** check final glyphs/subscripts, arrows, line crossings, whitespace, grayscale, and clipping according to the [technical baseline](figure-style.md). Apply the same semantic standards to complex and simple drawings rather than scoring node/panel counts.

Numerical research figures need real provenance chains. Prefer editable vector, TikZ/SVG, or Python workflows for precise schematics. Image models may support visual-concept and composition exploration under the brief above, but are not trustworthy generators of numerical curves, axes, data heatmaps, or exact technical topology. Verify final scientific relationships, exact labels, and dimensions in the editable drawing workflow. Deliver editable labels, scripts/vector sources, finished artwork, and captions, and accurately identify which outputs were actually rendered and inspected.

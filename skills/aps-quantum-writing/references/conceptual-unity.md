# Conceptual Unity: What Does This Paper Actually Resolve?

Use this reference before outlining and before delivering a complete manuscript. The goal is one clearly expressible, important scientific question and a central claim jointly supported by the evidence. Shared terminology and storytelling must not be used to package several correct but unrelated results as a single paper. For local editing, address only issues relevant to the passage; do not require a whole-manuscript grade.

## Before outlining: use a minimal argument record to decide what belongs

Extract one central question, a one-sentence supported answer, and the understanding that answer changes. For each major result, record “assumptions/task → conclusion → role in the central question.” Place necessary conditions next to the corresponding inference. A title cannot conceal differences in access model, geometry, limit, or resource accounting.

Results may have technical dependencies, or they may provide complementary evidence that is inseparable from answering the same question. Independently derived matching upper and lower bounds, constraints on one mechanism from different observables, and a prediction with an experimental test do not require one theorem to be used in proving the next. Shared keywords, belonging to quantum research, or using the same tool are insufficient to establish a connection.

Apply two tests. If one major result were deleted, what would the central answer lose? If almost nothing changes, consider removing it, making it supporting material, or developing it into a separate paper. If the manuscript were split into two papers, would each remain complete without damaging the central answer? If so, reorganize the scientific core before revising the narrative. Deleting an independent complementary result may remove optimality, mechanism identification, or a complete evidential case; do not split such results merely because their proofs are independent. Conceptual unity does not require a single method, a single data source, or an invented connecting theorem.

## Judge correctness, novelty, and importance separately

Correctness asks whether the proof or data support the conclusion under the stated assumptions. Mathematical novelty asks whether the structure or technique adds something new. Scientific importance asks what important new understanding, explanation, capability, or limitation follows. A new theorem or more complicated proof cannot by itself replace scientific significance. Familiar tools can also yield an important new answer.

Compare prior work at the level of the specific question, assumptions/access model, conclusions, resource or scale conventions, and new understanding. Finding many papers with the same keywords does not establish that the manuscript lacks novelty. A small bibliography, high citation counts, or a popular field are not novelty scores either. If the closest results remain unverified, state in the assessment that this comparison needs verification; do not turn uncertainty into a claim of no novelty.

The value of a toy or oracle model depends on what it reveals, whether its key structure explains the target problem, and within which model its conclusions hold. If an advantage depends on special access, compare the actual tasks and resources. When Grover search, amplitude amplification, or concentration inequalities are used, distinguish direct substitution into a standard tool from a nontrivial new construction, tight bound, implementable scheme, or physical insight. Neither dismiss a contribution automatically because its tools are familiar nor present a tool's name as the contribution.

When gaps remain, prioritize work that can change the central answer: a necessary new result, control, or connecting argument, or removal of material that departs from the main question. Narrative alone can improve the presentation of an existing scientific relationship. Propose a connecting theorem only if it is scientifically necessary, supported by the materials, and genuinely provable. Do not invent a dependency, theorem, or causal conclusion to create an appearance of unity.

## The brief assessment delivered with a complete manuscript

After checking the scientific content, figures, and layout, give the user a separate concise assessment. Do not insert it into the manuscript or attach a long scoring table by default:

1. **Unity, A–D:** A: strongly unified; B: substantially unified, with local connections needing improvement; C: a loose central thread; D: an assortment of results. Explain the specific relationships between results that determine the grade, rather than grading by section count.
2. **The one sentence worth remembering:** State the object/conditions, central conclusion, and new understanding. If such a sentence cannot yet be written, do not substitute a slogan.
3. **The paper's principal character:** Does it primarily solve an important problem, or primarily display several provable or computable results? Give one principal judgment and its key reservation. The two are not wholly exclusive; state when the evidence is still insufficient to decide.
4. **Upgrade priorities:** Rank changes by expected scientific effect. Identify whether the most important next step is key evidence or a new result, deletion or splitting, narrative reorganization, or establishing a genuine central connection. List only substantively necessary items.
5. **Journal positioning:** Use the question's importance, significance to broad readers, evidence strength, completeness, and current article-type requirements to describe the present fit and conditions still to be met. Distinguish an aspirational target from evidence-supported positioning; do not guarantee acceptance.

Before delivery, check that the title, abstract, and conclusion express this shared core; every major result in the main text has an explicit role; and comparison with the closest literature supports the judgment about new understanding. If the assessment reveals problems that can be corrected using the existing evidence, revise the manuscript before giving the final judgment. Leave only changes requiring new scientific input as specific next steps. A louder title is not a substitute.

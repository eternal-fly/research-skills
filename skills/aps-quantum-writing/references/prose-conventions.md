# Prose punctuation and literature wording

Apply these editorial preferences when drafting or revising narrative main text, appendices, End Matter, Supplemental Material, and their captions, including a local sentence edit. Follow the requested language. This is an author-selected style, not an APS prohibition or a claim about all journal articles.

## Compose the scientific relationship as a sentence

Use complete, connected sentences without colon punctuation or parenthetical/explanatory dashes in the prose. This includes ASCII and full-width colons, em dashes, en dashes used as sentence punctuation, and TeX or ASCII dash spellings such as `---` and `\textemdash`. Introduce an equation grammatically, or end the preceding sentence with a period. Express an explanation, contrast, consequence, or enumeration through suitable clauses, commas, semicolons, or separate sentences. Preserve the actual relationship rather than merely exchanging characters.

Keep ordinary word hyphens, numerical ranges, minus signs, and mathematical relations in their own roles. Mathematical notation such as `$f:A\to B$` and `$N_1:N_2=1:2$` is not prose punctuation. Preserve citation commands, label keys, URLs, code, and source syntax. Review rendered prose separately from these structures instead of applying a file-wide character deletion.

## Identify heading punctuation by its role

A dash joining an actual run-in heading to the first body sentence is allowed. Preserve the selected heading route, including current PRL `Heading—` and the separate `Note added.—` form. This does not permit a dash later in the body sentence or require adding headings. See [heading microtypography](microtypography.md).

For this no-colon prose style, avoid optional colon-ended secondary run-in headings, including `\apssecondaryrunin`. When the paragraph does not need a heading, fold the label into its opening sentence. When a substantive division is needed, choose an appropriate supported heading level while preserving the real hierarchy. Do not redefine the helper, change the documented journal convention, or add a section solely to evade punctuation. Required formatting in a supplied template remains a separate heading decision; flag an actual conflict outside the manuscript rather than silently changing the template. Appendix-title punctuation is also heading metadata, not punctuation inside its narrative text.

## Describe the contribution rather than its publication status

For a verified journal source, introduce the relevant finding as existing research, previous work, or an earlier study, with the corresponding citation. For an online manuscript with verified authors and a genuinely recent date, use recent work by those authors or the authors' recent study, again with the citation. Adapt these expressions naturally to the requested language rather than repeating a fixed label for every reference.

Do not put preprint numbers or publication-status descriptions such as "the published article", "the published paper", "the journal article", "the online preprint", or "the preprint" into the narrative. Connect the cited result to the question being discussed. Retain the distinction between a conjecture, theorem, fit, simulation, and experiment through the scientific claim itself.

If the author or date is unverified, use a neutral citation-based expression such as "The analysis in Ref.~\cite{key}" or "Earlier work" when chronology is known. Do not invent a name, call an old or undated source recent, or imply that a source has been peer reviewed. Accurate bibliography entries, preprint identifiers and version information stay in the reference list, BibTeX records, or a separate source-verification note. Preserve valid citation keys, citation placement and source traceability. These conventions do not authorize deleting a reference or changing its actual publication status.

## Short conference and journal manuscripts

When a short manuscript draws on a reference's fuller proof, calculation, discussion, or other additional material, retain the original scientific wording and the citation to the source that supports it. Do not add "extended version", "expanded version", "longer version", "full version", or equivalent labels in the requested language merely because the source contains more detail than the short format. Apply this to both the current manuscript and the cited work, including supporting-material prose. Describe the result or refer to the proof directly, without recasting an existing sentence as a statement about manuscript versions.

Preserve the cited result's conditions and the locator or citation key that identifies the actual supporting material. Do not attribute a proof to the short account when it appears only in the cited companion, or promise an unprovided Supplement. A genuine scientific extension, such as extending a Hamiltonian to additional couplings, remains scientific content. Actual source titles and version metadata in the bibliography remain accurate; this prose preference does not rename a reference or remove a required submission disclosure.

## Original revision example

This example assumes the source already establishes the stated finite-size result.

Before

> The published article establishes a restricted result: the gap is positive for every finite irreducible chain—the bound may depend on the size.

After

> Existing research establishes a positive gap for every finite irreducible chain, with a bound that may depend on the size.

Keep the original citation beside the claim. The revision preserves irreducibility, the finite-size quantifier, and the size dependence; it does not establish a uniform gap.

Before delivery, read the revised narrative and check sentence punctuation and literature wording. Then independently check that headings, mathematical symbols, citation commands, conditions, uncertainty, negative results, and reference metadata still have their intended meaning. A literal character match is a review aid, not a substitute for identifying those roles.
